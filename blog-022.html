Cashflow: Your Unit Economics Over a Time Horizon

Most founders learn unit economics as a formula. That formula is correct — but it's static. It shows you a margin at a point in time, not the shape of your cash as it actually moves. And the shape is where founders get surprised.

This post walks through why the per-unit P&L is only half the picture, and what the cashflow timeline of a single unit looks like when you account for when costs hit versus when revenue arrives.

The unit P&L — what it tells you and what it doesn't

Start with a $60 product. The standard unit P&L formula:

Profit = Selling Price − Buy Cost − Platform Fees − Logistics − Returns − Admin − Labor

Here's what those numbers look like filled in:

Line item Amount
Selling Price+$60.00
Buy Cost−$10.00
Shipping and Tariff−$10.00
Storage & Pick Pack−$2.00
Shipping to Customers−$10.00
Platform Fee (15%)−$9.00
Return (20%)−$3.00
Advertising (10%)−$6.00
Remaining Gross Profit$10.00

$10 margin on a $60 product. That's 17% — not the 67% margin you'd calculate if you only subtracted the $20 buy-plus-shipping cost from the selling price.

Most new sellers don't see the full picture until they're already in the red. The unit P&L above corrects that. It's a necessary starting point.

But it still has a blind spot: it doesn't tell you when the money moves.

A healthy margin on paper can still produce a cashflow crisis in practice. The timing of your outflows versus your inflows is what determines whether you can keep operating — especially when you scale.

The cashflow trap — the same $60 unit over time

The unit P&L stacks all costs and revenue in a single column as if they happen simultaneously. They don't.

Here's the same $60 product mapped across the months when each cash event actually occurs:

Line item Unit P&L M1 M2 M3 M4 M5 M6 M7
Buy Cost −$10.00 −$10
Ocean and Tariff −$10.00 −$10
Storage & Pick Pack −$2.00 −$1 −$1
Advertising (10%) −$6.00 −$2 −$2 −$2
Platform Fee (15%) −$9.00 −$9
Shipping to Customers −$10.00 −$10
Selling Price +$60.00 +$60
Return (20%) −$3.00 −$3
Cashflow per Unit −$10 −$22 −$25 −$47 +$13 +$10

Read across the bottom row. You place the order in Month 1. By Month 5 — before a single dollar of revenue arrives — you are $47 in the hole on this one unit. Revenue hits in Month 6. You're net positive by Month 7, at +$10.

That's a 7-month cashflow cycle on a single unit. The unit P&L says $10 profit. The cashflow timeline says you're down $47 before you see any of it.

Both numbers are true simultaneously. But only one of them threatens your ability to keep operating.

Why this matters when you scale

Now imagine you're not ordering one unit. You're ordering 1,000 — a reasonable opening order for a new product.

At 1,000 units, the $47 peak cashflow exposure becomes $47,000 tied up before any revenue arrives. If you're launching in Month 1 and reordering to stay in stock, you may be placing your next purchase order while still waiting on revenue from the first batch.

This is the unit economics trap: the business model is profitable, but the timing of costs versus revenue creates a capital requirement that catches founders off guard. The faster you grow, the deeper the hole before it pays back.

The model doesn't break. But you can run out of cash inside a working model.

Scaling a profitable unit model without understanding its cashflow cycle is like driving with the right destination but no fuel gauge. You can be on the right road and still run out of runway.

Breakeven and runway — the two numbers that follow

Once you understand the cashflow shape of a unit, two numbers become critical operational metrics.

Breakeven — how many units before you're profitable?

Formula: Fixed Costs ÷ Margin Per Unit = Breakeven Units

Example:

  • $5,000 monthly fixed costs
  • $10 margin per unit
  • = 500 units/month to break even

If you can't reach 500 units at your current economics, fix the model before adding volume. More units into a broken model accelerates the loss — it doesn't resolve it.

Runway — how many months until you run out of cash?

Formula: Cash on Hand ÷ Monthly Losses = Runway (months)

Example:

  • $15,000 in savings
  • Burn rate of −$2,000/month
  • = 7.5 months of runway

Runway is not a comfort metric — it's a planning constraint. Every milestone (first sale, breakeven, reorder cycle) needs to land inside your runway window. If it doesn't, you need more capital, a faster path to breakeven, or both.

Know your runway before you commit to an order size. The question isn't just "can I afford this order?" — it's "can I afford this order and everything between now and when the revenue arrives?"

What to model before you commit capital

Before placing an order — especially a first order — you need three things built out:

1. The full unit P&L.

Not just sell price minus buy cost. Every line: platform fees, freight, storage, returns, advertising, and labor allocation. This is the $10 margin in the example above.

2. The cashflow timeline per unit.

Map when each cost actually hits your account versus when revenue arrives. Your real capital exposure is the deepest negative point on that curve — not the final margin.

3. Your breakeven and runway.

At your target order size and projected sell-through rate, when do you break even? Does that fall within your cash runway? If the answer is no, the plan needs to change before the order goes in.

These three models take time to build once. They save founders from the most common and most preventable form of cash crisis: running out of money inside a business model that actually works.

Unit economics ratios tell you whether your model makes money in theory. The cashflow timeline tells you whether you survive long enough to prove it in practice.

The $60 product in this example is profitable. It's also $47 in the hole for most of its first cycle. Both of those things are true simultaneously — and understanding that gap is what separates founders who plan from founders who get surprised.

If you're mapping this for the first time, start with one product, one order cycle, and the full cost breakdown above. The shape will tell you more than any margin percentage.
